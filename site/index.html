Creating Content for Media

About the project.

Howard Xu · Creative portfolio

This portfolio responds to Option 2, “A particular media product,” through an independent fan music video for “Suito” and two planned promotional pieces. The main work combines landscapes, light, movement and illustrated characters to interpret changes in the music.

I created all the 3D blockout guides, wrote the prompts and completed the After Effects editing. The production combines 3D rendering, AI-assisted image generation and video editing, and compositing. Music and third-party assets are credited separately.

The intended audience includes Rotaeno players and viewers interested in rhythm-game music and animation. YouTube is the planned setting for the complete video. An Instagram Reel will introduce its movement and atmosphere, while a separate feed image will present its title and visual identity.

The works.

One complete viewing experience, a short introduction and a single-image identity. The final film is available; the teaser and promotional image are planned.

01 / Creative processScene planning

Space, cameras & blockouts.

The project began with editable scenes: flowers, forest corridors, a city, birds and an energy form. Geometry and camera paths established the composition before surface detail was developed.

The early storyboard organised six environments into sixteen shots. I developed the scene order and camera movement around changes in the music, then refined the structure as the imagery took shape.

My contribution covers the 3D blockout guides, prompt writing and After Effects editing. The guide scenes established positions, depth and camera movement, while the game artwork supplied visual references.

Why keep the guide editable?

Scene structure, motion and appearance could be reviewed separately. Timeline camera markers organise the camera changes (Blender Foundation, n.d.).

02 / Creative processWorkflow design

Testing the generation workflow.

I explored how depth guides, text and reference images could work together. Comparing the workflow designs helped me separate camera and scene structure from the appearance of the generated image.

Depth + text

The guide depth sequence supplies spatial information, while the text describes the intended appearance.

Depth + text + reference

The same depth guide is combined with an opening-shot image to develop a more consistent visual treatment.

The two r33 workflow designs share a nine-second depth guide. Adding an image reference changes how surface detail and style are described, giving me another way to develop visual continuity across shots.

Research connection

Spatial conditioning has a precedent in image-generation research: Zhang et al. (2023) demonstrate controls such as depth, edges and pose. This research provides a useful way to discuss scene structure and visual appearance as separate creative decisions.

03 / Creative processMotion & atmosphere

The kingfisher & the fire.

The bird sequence uses a 3D guide for the perch, camera and flight path, with separate references for foliage and the kingfisher’s appearance.

Intended

Three readable head turns; a hard cut at 5.5 seconds; flight towards the upper right at approximately seven seconds; fire spreading through the forest.

Observed in v29b

More natural foliage, but unwanted wide water and waterfall features entered the composition. The three head turns were unclear, fire started late and the cut was one frame early.

Prompt study · translated from Chinese

“@Image1 supplies only the natural, detailed cinematic CG style and materials; retain low, gently sloping stream banks. @Image2 supplies only the kingfisher’s appearance.”
Read the full v29b English translation+

Edit @Video1. Replace the empty land on both banks with a lush broadleaf forest, fully present from the opening. Place varied trees below the main tree, along the right stream bank and across the lower edge revealed as the camera pulls back. Layer near, middle and distant crowns, maintain a continuous distant forest edge and leave gaps that reveal the narrow stream. Vary tree heights, use natural branching and full lower and middle foliage, and form crown edges from small, thin leaves. Densely cover the understorey with grasses, ferns, shrubs and moss.

Retain the video's camera position, movement, terrain, bird and perch locations. Both shots show the same forest: tree groups stay rooted in consistent positions and retain their size. Leave space around the bird and perch. @Image1 supplies only the natural, detailed cinematic CG style and materials; retain low, gently sloping stream banks. @Image2 supplies only the kingfisher's appearance. Keep the visual treatment consistent across both shots.

The first half is sunny and free of fire. The kingfisher grips the branch with folded wings. It first turns its head towards the camera side, pauses to look, turns towards the stream on the right and then looks back towards the camera side. Make the changes in beak direction clear while keeping the body steady.

Retain the hard cut at 5.5 seconds, followed by the camera pulling back and rising. Fire starts in the distant forest. At approximately 7 seconds, the kingfisher flaps away towards the upper right along its original trajectory. After it leaves, fire spreads from the main tree's roots towards the perch and crown.

Towards the end, the burning areas on the near bank, opposite bank and distant forest connect. Flames roll through branches and crowns; grasses, ferns and shrubs burn in succession. All visible vegetation is burning or charred, while blackened crown shapes and forest depth remain readable. Smoke rises, but trees can still be distinguished through its gaps. The sky becomes slightly grey while remaining daytime, and the stream keeps flowing. Retain the original duration and speed. No audio.

What the test made visible

The test showed how strongly a reference image could influence the landscape. More precise instructions were needed to preserve the narrow stream, make the head turns readable and bring the fire in at the intended point.

04 / Creative processPrompt revision

Revising the energy sequence.

A three-camera guide became the clearest iteration case: keep the underlying scene, revise the environment instructions and compare the generated result at the same time point.

The v18 revision removed extra ground decorations while retaining terrain, rocks and energy animation. The v17 and v20 tests then used the same v18 guide and reference order. Four environment instructions changed together: sky brightness, ember placement, tree density and rock emission.

Request → visible result

No rock self-emission
Unwanted red glow reduced in sampled v20 frames.
Darker sky
The exposed sky was still brighter than intended.
Embers on burnt wood and ground
Embers became more visible; attachment detail remained a review point.
Readable absorption and core growth
Core growth remained difficult to read in the test.

Prompt study · translated from Chinese

“Embers belong to charred wood and charcoal: neither rock should glow red or emit its own light. There must be no open flames or broad bands of fire.”
Read the full v20 English translation+

Edit @Video1: retain the existing energy form, convergence, core growth and original three camera moves. Replace the grey-white environment with a burnt forest at night after a wildfire has gone out.

Keep the video's asymmetric three-dimensional energy form, with depth at both front and back. The head, body and tail of each cluster of light must continuously converge along their original trajectories and be absorbed into the centre, with new clusters entering from all three-dimensional directions. Do not use static overlapping layers, opacity fades or rotation of the whole mass as a substitute. The core is a dense, opaque, irregular warm-white luminous mass that gradually grows as it absorbs energy. Curved bright veins continuously roll inside it; there is no metallic spherical shell, cracking or hard reflective surface. Retain pale-gold translucent outer light, variations in width, broken filaments and soft glow. The form must retain volume in side and overhead views rather than becoming a thin wire skeleton. @Image2 supplies the optical quality of the energy; the video governs its form and movement.

Retain the terrain and both rocks. Keep a surrounding burnt forest of matte-black, charred broadleaf trees at slightly lower density. Widen the spacing between near- and middle-distance trunks moderately, reduce overlapping forked branches and leave readable gaps between trees. Retain a continuous distant forest layer. Include a few fallen trunks and root stumps. Charred grass-root mats, broken stems, fern remains, burnt leaves and charcoal fragments cover most of the ground. Dark-red embers are clearly visible inside trunk cracks, broken branch ends, root cavities and pockets of charcoal on the ground. Local orange-red hot spots slowly brighten and dim out of phase beneath ashy surfaces, faintly lighting nearby wood grain and ash. Occasional sparks rise from the ember cores, drift gently and fade. Embers belong to charred wood and charcoal: neither rock should glow red or emit its own light. There must be no open flames or broad bands of fire. Lower the sky brightness further to deep blue-grey black, retaining only faint cool tonal layers and readable treetop silhouettes; avoid a large bright blue-grey background. The energy remains the main local light source. Embers must be readable but dimmer than the energy. Keep exposure stable throughout. @Image1 is a reference only for natural cinematic CG materials and their scale.

Keep the ground-level opening and direct hard cuts at approximately 5.17 and 8.08 seconds, without whip pans or transitions. All three shots share the same scene, energy and continuous time. Retain the complete duration and original speed. No audio.

I divided the energy sequence into three shots, enhanced their detail and motion, then brought them into AE for timing and compositing.

05 / Creative processLayered movement

A quieter character sequence.

The warm character illustration followed a separate image-repair and motion-test branch, with a locked composition and layered movement.

The H3 tests explored sampling resolution, refinement scale and motion stability. Increasing the refinement scale brought out more detail, but keeping the characters, hands and painted texture consistent remained central to the animation.

A separate Seedance prompt study proposed a ten-second loop. It assigned different movement ranges to bodies, hair, clothing and vegetation, with slower motion and offset rhythms to avoid uniform swaying.

Prompt study · translated from Chinese

“Different leaf clusters and flowers vary in amplitude, direction and peak timing, out of phase with the characters; avoid identical, synchronous swaying.”
Read the full v5 English translation+

Use the input illustration as the visual reference. Preserve the characters' identities, clothing, forest composition, delicate linework, peach-gold backlight and painted texture. Create a ten-second, single-shot animation loop with a locked camera. Slightly restrain the movement range while keeping it clearly visible, with slow, long-period rhythms.

From 0 to 10 seconds, a gentle wind passes through the forest. The two characters retain their positions and relationship. Their upper bodies rise, fall and sway softly with restrained amplitude; head and neck movement is smaller than torso movement. Breathing and shifts of weight remain visible. The arms follow coherently. The central hands stay touching and overlapping, with complete five-finger anatomy on the extended palm. Keep the light orb's outline, size and brightness stable.

The middle sections and tips of long hair rise, extend and settle softly in coherent locks. Slightly restrain their spread and keep the roots anchored. Sleeves, hems, ribbons and tassels extend and swing back with moderately restrained movement, smooth inertia and delay. Small ornaments move less. Keep this motion away from faces and the central hands.

Plants move independently according to material and depth. Foreground leaf clusters and thin branches slowly bend and sway with moderately restrained amplitude, still slightly greater than the characters' torsos. Flowers and fine grasses move softly with smaller amplitude; flower heads lag slightly behind their stems. Middle-distance leaves move less, and distant crowns only undulate gently. Main trunks and thick branches stay fixed. Different leaf clusters and flowers vary in amplitude, direction and peak timing, out of phase with the characters; avoid identical, synchronous swaying. Existing suspended leaves travel back and forth on slightly narrower arcs.

Each group completes one long-period cycle within ten seconds. Give the rise and settling phases time to develop and retain these layered amplitudes. Reduce frequent reversals, short rebounds and fine jitter. Match composition and movement phase between the first and last frames, with consistent direction and speed across the seam. Keep movement continuous without pauses or freeze frames.

Camera position, focal length, composition, perspective, focus, colour and exposure remain constant. No captions, new effects or audio.

06 / Creative processColour & contrast

Light, colour & attention.

The grading studies explored how a darker environment and stronger embers could hold attention around the luminous energy form.

Environment

Reduce the surroundings’ middle tones so that the core remains the brightest local form.

Embers

Increase warm orange-red detail while preserving a readable relationship between burnt vegetation and energy.

The AfterBurn study uses Lumetri adjustments and a gradual opening exposure change. I compared stills across the sequence to balance the dark surroundings, warm embers and luminous centre. A separate SkyDark study explored the sky’s brightness.

Technical reference

Lumetri provides controls for colour, contrast and light directly in the AE timeline (Adobe, 2025). I used these controls to explore the relationship between the background and the energy form.

This portfolio develops a fan music video for “Suito” and plans two promotional pieces around it. The main video interprets musical changes through landscapes, fire, light and character imagery. Its intended audience includes Rotaeno players and people interested in rhythm-game music and animation. An English description introduces the work to viewers unfamiliar with the game. Across the three pieces, the aim is to make the project recognisable and give viewers a clear route from a short introduction to the complete video.

I created all the 3D blockout guides, wrote the prompts and completed the After Effects editing. The blockouts established camera views, spatial relationships and the placement of important forms. They gave me a reference for assessing the generated material: I could compare the scene structure with the guide and identify changes in lighting or detail. I used AI tools for image generation and video editing, then selected and assembled the shots in After Effects. Separate credits identify the music and source assets.

The energy sequence shows how this process developed. Its v18 blockout retained the terrain, rocks, energy movement and three camera views while removing added ground decorations. The v17 generation introduced unwanted red light beneath a rock. For v20, I specified that the rocks should have no self-emission and assigned embers to burnt wood and ground features. Sampled frames showed less red glow on the rocks, but the sky remained too bright and the energy core's growth was still unclear. The revision improved one relationship within the image while leaving two others unresolved. In another test, I would keep the guide and reference order fixed, then change one environmental instruction at a time. This would make each revision's effect easier to evaluate.

In AE, I adjusted shots within the full sequence. The composition includes three energy clips with time remapping, alongside transitions, overlays and credits. Separate grading studies explored darker surroundings and stronger embers, directing attention towards the luminous centre. These stages addressed different aspects of the image: the guide controlled structure, the prompt described its treatment, and the edit placed it within the music. Playback is the next check for continuity, musical timing and the transition between neighbouring shots.

Gibbs et al. (2015) describe platform vernacular through the relationship between platform architecture and shared practices. This helps explain the planned roles of YouTube and Instagram. YouTube will hold the complete landscape video. The Instagram teaser needs its own short viewing arc, reframed subjects and readable text, while the feed image needs an immediately identifiable title and focal point. I will assess these pieces at phone scale and check whether their viewing instructions are clear. The results will inform the final account of platform adaptation.

Aaker's (1997) brand-personality framework offers a vocabulary for the project's intended visual character. Excitement is a useful aim for the energy sequence's movement and contrasting light. I will carry selected colours, motifs and title styling into the promotional pieces so that they remain recognisable together. This also presents a design challenge: a detailed image that works across several seconds may become difficult to read in a small, static post. The promotional layout therefore needs a more selective visual focus.

Audience testing would help me assess whether the work communicates as intended. I would ask viewers to identify the project, describe the teaser's main impression and locate the full video. Watching the work on a phone would also reveal whether focal points, captions and the end card remain readable at a smaller scale. Their responses could guide changes to framing and pacing, and help me judge how effectively the promotional pieces introduce the full film.

The next stage is to complete the teaser and promotional image, review the chosen main export, and record the changes made during that review. My main lesson is to compare intentions with visible results throughout production. Keeping guides, prompts and edits together makes those decisions easier to trace and gives each later revision a clear starting point.
